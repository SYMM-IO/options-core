Accounting kernels and plugin authority

The question: when a small core holds everyone's money and pluggable product modules tell it what to move, what stops one module from taking money that belongs to another module's users?

I read six kernels from source and one pair of app-chains from docs. Derive v2 was covered earlier and is only used for comparison. Everything below was checked on 2026-10-03 against these commits.

Short answer

The kernels bound plugins in three ways:

  1. Opt-in scope. A plugin can only touch funds a user attached to it: an EVC controller, a Morpho market, a Uniswap or Balancer pool.
  2. Caps in the kernel. Aave v4 caps what each spoke can draw. Synthetix v3 caps what each market can withdraw.
  3. Net-zero at the end of a call. Uniswap v4 and Balancer v3 let the plugin compute anything, then revert unless every balance is settled.

The failure mode repeats. Wherever the kernel accepts a number the plugin reports, and that number feeds shared accounting, the cap stops being a cap. Synthetix's reportedDebt and Aave v4's premium reporting are both documented as ways a malicious module can hurt users outside it.

Euler Ethereum Vault Connector (EVC)

Mechanism. The EVC holds no funds. It authenticates callers and schedules checks, and ERC-4626 vaults do the custody (whitepaper, Introduction; EVC Contract Privileges). Each address owns 256 sub-accounts that share its first 19 bytes, and the owner is recorded on first use (whitepaper, Authorisation). call, batch and controlCollateral defer status checks to the end of the outermost call. Then the EVC calls checkAccountStatus on the account's controller and checkVaultStatus on each vault that asked for a check, with at most 10 of each per batch (specs CER-43, CER-45, CER-73). permit executes EIP-712 signed calldata, and owner-set nonce namespaces scope those signatures.

Authority. A user who calls enableController is, in Euler's words, "entirely submitting the account to the rules encoded in the controller vault's code" (whitepaper, Controller). controlCollateral checks only that the caller is the account's single enabled controller and that the target is one of the account's enabled collaterals (source). The EVC does not limit the amount. The collateral vault sees an ordinary withdraw. The bounds:

  • The user opts in.
  • An account can have only one controller when it is checked.
  • Only the controller can call disableController.
  • A controller cannot use permit to seize collateral or release itself (CER-21, CER-57).

Operators are revocable by the owner at any time. A controller is not (whitepaper, Operators). Lockdown mode stops the owner's own operators and calls, but "authorized controllers can still control collaterals for the accounts even under lockdown" (whitepaper, Lockdown Mode).

Upgradeability. The EVC has no admin. Its onlyOwner modifiers refer to account owners. On top of it, the Euler Vault Kit lets each vault creator choose an upgradeable beacon proxy or an immutable proxy, and lets a governor renounce control (EVK whitepaper). EVK also ramps liquidation LTV changes over a duration set by the governor. A governor cannot liquidate borrowers with a sudden parameter change (setLTV, LTV Ramping).

Findings. The yAudit review of EVC and EVK (March 25 to May 5, 2024) found two issues that show where the risk sits (report):

  • Critical. deloop() never asked the EVC to check the paying account, which allowed bad debt between two vaults that accept each other as collateral. The EVC only checks what a plugin asks it to check.
  • High. A malicious controller enabled by the attacker could re-enter a savings vault during the status-check callback.

The report marks both fixed. I found no public exploit of the EVC itself.

Synthetix v3

Mechanism. Accounts are NFTs with role-based permissions. LPs delegate collateral to pools. A pool owner points the pool's credit at markets with setPoolConfiguration, giving each market a weight and a maxDebtShareValueD18 (MarketConfiguration). A market is any contract that implements IMarket: name, reportedDebt and minimumCredit (IMarket). depositMarketUsd burns snxUSD and raises the market's creditCapacityD18. withdrawMarketUsd mints snxUSD, but only up to getWithdrawableMarketUsd, which equals credit capacity plus collateral the market deposited (source, withdraw).

Authority and caps.

  • A market's cash draw is capped by credit capacity.
  • When a market's debt per share passes a pool's maxDebtShareValue, that pool is disconnected from the market.
  • The system owner caps how much collateral each market may deposit (configureMaximumMarketCollateral).
  • minimumCredit lets a market stop LPs from leaving.

Debt is the weak point. The core takes reportedDebt on trust and spreads it to the pools. SIP-303 says it plainly: "a malicious actor could generate a market, persuade pools to provide it with liquidity, and then set the balance value to a large negative number … This would subject the pools involved to liquidation" (SIP-303).

Perps and spot. The perps market registers once and reports the debt of its whole book. It computes that debt from its own collateral and prices (reportedDebt, minimumCredit). The spot market registers one market per synth in createSynth (source). Product knowledge also leaked into the core. AccountRBAC defines PERPS_MODIFY_COLLATERAL and similar permission constants (source).

Upgradeability and status. The core is a UUPS proxy that the owner can upgrade (UpgradeModule). Synthetix shut down its L2 deployments in 2025 (blog, 2025-08-16). I did not verify which v3 deployments still run.

Uniswap v4 PoolManager

Mechanism. unlock calls the caller's unlockCallback. Inside it, every swap, liquidity change, take, settle, mint, burn or clear adds a signed delta per (currency, address) in transient storage. NonzeroDeltaCount tracks how many deltas are non-zero, and unlock reverts with CurrencyNotSettled unless the count is zero at the end (source). Payment is measured, not claimed: sync snapshots the balance, the payer transfers, and settle credits the difference (source).

Authority. A hook's permissions are 14 bits of its address, and the PoolKey fixes which hook a pool uses (Hooks.sol). The whitepaper notes this "ensures that even upgradeable hooks obey certain invariants" (whitepaper). Four *_RETURNS_DELTA flags let a hook change amounts, and the change is booked to the hook's own address. The PoolManager stores no per-pool token balances, only price, liquidity, ticks and positions (Pool.State). Pools are isolated by liquidity math and by position ownership. The kernel does not protect a swapper from a hook in the pool they chose. Uniswap's own list says a return-delta hook "can 'take' all specified token without crediting the user", which "should be checked in a router" (Known Effects of Hook Permissions).

Upgradeability. The contract cannot be upgraded. The owner only picks a fee controller, and the protocol fee is capped at 0.1% (source).

Incidents. Cork lost about $12M on 2025-05-28 because its hook's beforeSwap did not check msg.sender (Cork post-mortem). Bunni lost about $8.4M on 2025-09-02 to a rounding error in its own withdrawal logic (Bunni post-mortem). I took both amounts from third-party analyses. Both were bugs inside the plugin, and neither report blames the PoolManager.

Balancer v3 Vault

Mechanism. Balancer v3 uses the same pattern as Uniswap v4: unlock, a transient modifier that reverts with BalanceNotSettled if _nonZeroDeltaCount is non-zero, and settle priced from the change in _reservesOf (source). Unlike Uniswap v4, the Vault also keeps per-pool token balances in _poolTokenBalances (source).

Authority. Hooks only change amounts when enableHookAdjustedAmounts is set. When it is, the Vault itself checks the hook's result against the user's limit and reverts with HookAdjustedSwapLimit (source). Uniswap leaves that check to the router. Recovery mode is "a safe proportional withdrawal, with no external calls", an exit that never runs pool or hook code (IVaultAdmin).

Upgradeability. "The system cannot be upgraded" (README). The power to pause expires: the window is at most 4 years plus a 180-day buffer (source).

Context. The 2025-11-03 exploit hit v2 Composable Stable Pools and not v3 (Balancer post-mortem; I read it through secondary summaries). On 2026-09-15 a wind-down was proposed that moves pools to withdrawal-only mode through pause and recovery mode. My source is press coverage (crypto.news), and I have not verified the vote result.

Aave v4 (Hub and Spokes)

Mechanism. The Hub "is immutable" and has no oracle. Spokes are upgradeable. They hold user positions, oracles and liquidation logic (overview). Spokes call add, remove, draw, restore, reportDeficit, refreshPremium, eliminateDeficit and transferShares. Aave v4 went live on Ethereum on 2026-03-30 with "conservative supply and borrow caps" that the DAO will raise (Aave blog).

Authority and caps. Each spoke has a SpokeConfig {addCap, drawCap, riskPremiumThreshold, active, halted} (source). In the Hub code (remove/draw, validation):

  • draw checks the spoke's debt plus any reported deficit against drawCap.
  • remove subtracts from spoke.addedShares with checked arithmetic, so a spoke cannot remove more than it added.
  • reportDeficit cannot exceed what the spoke drew.

ChainSecurity's March 2026 audit sets the limits of those caps (report, §2.2–2.3). "The Hub does not perform any solvency checks on Spokes or users". "A malicious Spoke could inflate the amount of premium shares … allowing the malicious Spoke to drain the Hub liquidity for that asset". Users "must not only trust the Hub but also all other Spokes connected to that Hub that have at least one common asset". The current Hub requires premiumShares <= drawnShares × riskPremiumThreshold (source). That limits the inflation. I did not confirm the check was added because of the audit.

Morpho Blue

Mechanism. A market's ID is the hash of MarketParams {loanToken, collateralToken, oracle, irm, lltv}. Anyone can call createMarket with an IRM and LLTV from lists that governance controls. Governance can only add to those lists (source). One contract holds every token, and each market keeps separate totals.

Authority. The oracle and IRM are fixed for the life of the market. Governance "cannot halt the operation of a market or modify its LLTV, IRM, and oracle", and it can set a fee of at most 25% of interest (whitepaper, §1.2 and §3.4; MAX_FEE). createMarket lists the assumptions about tokens, oracle and IRM that keep the market safe and live (source). User delegation is all or nothing: an address in isAuthorized can act on every market (source).

Incident. In October 2024 a PAXG/USDC market launched with a mis-scaled oracle lost about $230k. The loss stayed inside that market (Morpho statement; the amount comes from secondary analyses).

dYdX v4 and Hyperliquid, briefly

In dYdX v4, x/clob reaches balances through a narrow SubaccountsKeeper interface that it declares itself (source). That interface holds CanUpdateSubaccounts and UpdateSubaccounts, tagged with an UpdateType such as Match, Transfer or Withdrawal, plus a few fee and insurance-fund transfers. The subaccounts module runs the collateral checks (source). All modules ship in one binary, so no plugin is hostile. On Hyperliquid, HyperEVM contracts read HyperCore through precompiles and write through CoreWriter at 0x3333…3333. They act as an ordinary account, and order actions are "delayed onchain for a few seconds" (docs, page not versioned). Product contracts there get no power over anyone else.

What this means for options-core

(a) Minimal kernel interface

Keep the vault from the synthesis note, with one change: a module never gets power over "all collateral" the way an EVC controller does. It gets power only over amounts committed to it.

type PoolId is bytes32; // owner (20 bytes) | index (12 bytes); the owner is read from the id, no registry

interface IKernel {
	// pool owner or a revocable operator
	function deposit(PoolId pool, address token, uint256 amount) external;
	function withdraw(PoolId pool, address token, uint256 amount, address to) external;
	function commit(PoolId pool, address controller, bytes32 key, address token, uint256 amount) external;

	// controller only: every debit must come from escrow[msg.sender][fromKey]
	struct Leg { bytes32 fromKey; bytes32 toKey; address token; uint256 amount; }
	function move(Leg[] calldata legs) external;
	function release(bytes32 key, PoolId to, address token, uint256 amount) external;
}

Each leg moves an amount from one bucket to another, so a batch nets to zero by construction. There is no need for Uniswap-style transient deltas. Add an unlock callback only if a module later needs external calls in the middle of settlement, such as swapping collateral. The main invariant for each token is that free balances plus escrows equal the kernel's token balance. EVC and Aave v4 both have Certora verification reports on their kernels (EVC audits, Aave v4 audits), and this invariant is the natural target for ours. Deposits should credit the measured balance change, as sync/settle does.

(b) Capping a module's authority

  1. Isolation by commitment. The options module can only debit escrow committed to it. A perps user who never committed to it has nothing it can reach. Morpho and Balancer use the same idea, per market and per pool.
  2. No reported numbers. The kernel should have no reportDebt, mint, share price or loss socialization. Synthetix and Aave v4 both show how a reported number defeats a cap. If Symmio later wants shared LP backing, build it as its own controller with its own escrow. The kernel still sees only moves.
  3. Caps belong where liquidity is shared. Aave needs caps in the Hub because spokes draw other people's money. Bilateral escrow does not have that problem. Put rollout caps in the controller, as immutable per-version parameters, rather than in an admin role on the kernel.
  4. Release only by the controller, with an exit at the controller layer. This matches EVC's disableController rule and gives the solver its guarantee. The cost is that a buggy controller can freeze funds; it cannot steal from other modules. The kernel cannot offer a Balancer-style recovery mode, because a bilateral escrow has no fair split that is independent of the module. The exit has to be the channel's challenge path, so the channel controller must be small and immutable.

(c) Immutability and upgradeability

Every newer kernel here is immutable: EVC, Morpho, the Uniswap v4 PoolManager, the Balancer v3 Vault and the Aave v4 Hub. Only Synthetix v3's core can be upgraded. My recommendation:

  • Make the kernel immutable, with no owner.
  • Make controllers and adjudicators immutable per version, and pin the controller address in each commitment, the way a PoolKey pins a hook and MarketParams pins an oracle.
  • If a controller is a proxy, show it in the interface, as EVK does with its upgradeable flag.
  • If any emergency power exists, make it expire, like Balancer's pause window, and never let it block exits.

The trade-off is that a kernel bug means redeploying and migrating. That is acceptable only if the kernel stays small. Morpho's whitepaper puts its core at about 650 lines (§3.4).

(d) Single-token or multi-token

Synthetix accounts in one unit, snxUSD, and that forces an oracle manager into the core. The other kernels keep balances per token and leave pricing to plugins: EVC to controllers, Aave to spokes, Morpho to each market's oracle. Key balances by token, give the kernel no oracle, and check net-zero per token. Version 1 can accept only USDC in the controller without changing the kernel interface. Cross-collateral margin belongs in a controller or adjudicator. The trade-off is lower capital efficiency for users who hold several tokens. In return, the kernel needs no oracle, and an oracle-free kernel is what makes immutability realistic.

The single most important implication: a module can only debit funds that users explicitly committed to it, and the kernel never accepts a module-reported number that moves anyone else's money.
