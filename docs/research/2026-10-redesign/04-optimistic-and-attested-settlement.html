Optimistic, attested, and proven settlement

Document metadata

title: Optimistic, attested, and proven settlement, and what a posted root can guarantee
tags:
    - symmio
    - options-core
    - research
date: 2026-10-03
status: research note

User and solver trade off-chain, and every few minutes someone posts a hash to the chain. Details come out only in a dispute. This note asks what that hash proves, and what each way of backing it up adds. Sources were checked on 2026-10-03, and anything I could not confirm from a primary source is marked UNVERIFIED. Nitro, Celer, ERC-7824, Derive, DLCs, Perennial and StarkEx are in the earlier note.

The short answer

A hash on-chain proves that someone committed to some data before a certain block. It says nothing about whether the data is correct, complete, or the newest version. Each system below adds one thing: the counterparty's signature, a window for objections, a third party's signature, or a proof. None of them helps a party who has lost the underlying data. A validity proof can show a computation is correct, but no proof can show that a newer signed state does not exist.

Across: an optimistic root every 30 minutes

Across is the closest working example of "post a root every N minutes". Relayers pay users on the destination chain from their own funds, then a dataworker proposes a root bundle to the HubPool on Ethereum with three Merkle roots. poolRebalanceRoot moves LP funds to spoke pools, relayerRefundRoot repays relayers, and slowRelayRoot fills deposits no relayer took (HubPool.sol). Since v3 the refund root also returns expired deposits (UMIP-179). After the liveness period anyone can execute leaves with Merkle proofs.

I read the HubPool at block 26,109,921. Liveness is 1,800 seconds and the bond is 0.45 ABT. ABT is a WETH9 copy with a proposer allowlist, so proposing is permissioned and disputing is open (BondToken.sol). The docs are stale. They say bundles go out "every 1.5 hours minimum" (relayer docs). In the previous 7,200 blocks there were 45 proposals, one every 158 blocks or so, about 32 minutes.

A dispute deletes the pending bundle and sends a yes or no question to UMA's SkinnyOptimisticOracle, judged by the rules in UMIP-157 and UMIP-179. A new proposal can go in at once. A dispute delays refunds by about one cycle, and the slow UMA vote only decides who keeps the bonds. The system never waits on its slowest judge, which is worth copying.

The guarantee is narrow. A bad bundle is stopped if one watcher recomputes it from public chain data and disputes within 30 minutes. The bond is not sized to the value at stake. Safety comes from public data and open disputes. The HubPool has logged 174 RootBundleDisputed events, 156 of them from the address that proposes every bundle today, which suggests Across's own bot cancels its bad bundles. That reading is UNVERIFIED. The owner also deleted two proposals on 2026-07-17, cause UNVERIFIED, so governance is a backstop too. Across v4 uses ZK proofs only to carry roots to other chains, and UMA still decides validity (v4 docs).

UMA Optimistic Oracle v3: what "undisputed" means

assertTruth takes a claim, bond, liveness and optional escalation manager. If nobody disputes in time, settleAssertion resolves the claim as true without ever reading it (OptimisticOracleV3.sol). A disputer matches the bond, and the loser's bond goes to the winner less a 50% burn. On the mainnet contract the default liveness is 7,200 seconds and the minimum USDC bond is 500 USDC.

The escalation manager matters most for us. It can block assertions, restrict disputers, and arbitrate disputes itself (EscalationManagerInterface.sol). The OOv3 source says this "lets integrations 'unplug' the DVM". Otherwise disputes go to the DVM, a token vote with 24-hour commit and reveal phases (UMA docs). It needs 65% of staked UMA and a 5M UMA quorum, and voters lose 0.1% of stake per wrong or missed vote (DVM 2.0). UMA's security claim is that corruption "is more expensive than the potential profit from doing so" (FAQ). The bond only stops spam. The ceiling on safety is the cost of corrupting the vote.

That ceiling has been tested on subjective questions. In March 2025 a large UMA holder pushed a Polymarket market on a Ukraine minerals deal to YES, and Polymarket declined refunds because "this wasn't a market failure" (The Block, secondary source). In July 2025 the Zelensky suit market resolved NO amid wide dispute (CoinDesk, secondary source). The fix was whitelisted proposers on a managed oracle, upgradeable by "a 2/2 multisig consisting of Polymarket and Risk Labs" (UMIP-189), with disputes still open (UMA blog). An optimistic claim is only as good as what it escalates to. Option payoffs are computable, so ours should escalate to code, never to a vote.

Optimistic rollups: why the window is a week

Arbitrum BoLD. Validators post assertions. A challenger bisects the disputed history down to one step and proves it on L1. BoLD is "all-vs-all", so one honest party can defend against any number of attackers (gentle introduction). It went live on 2025-02-12 (adoption page). The challenge period is 45,818 L1 blocks, about 6.4 days (docs), and disputes end within "two challenge periods, plus a two-day grace period for the Security Council". Bonds are 3,600 ETH per assertion (AIP).

OP Stack. A FaultDisputeGame bisects to one MIPS step on a 3.5-day chess clock per side, then waits a 3.5-day airgap in which the Guardian can blacklist it. Withdrawals wait about 7 days in total (configurability spec, bridge integration). Optimism fell back to permissioned games around the 2024 Granite upgrade (proposal) and in 2025 disclosed a bug that could lead to "permissionless dispute games resolving incorrectly under certain conditions" (disclosure).

Why a week. The window is not about how long the game takes. The BoLD paper sets it to "at least Cmax + D", where Cmax, the longest an attacker can censor challengers, is "on the order of about a week" (arXiv 2404.10491). On 2026-04-30 L2BEAT cut its Stage 1 minimum to 5 days, "one day to resolve economic attacks, and 4 days to resolve network delays", keeping 7 for Stage 2 (L2BEAT forum). Vitalik argues "1-2 days is enough to deal with any problem other than a 51% censorship attack" (Ethereum Magicians).

On an L2. Options-core would inherit the L2's own censorship delay. A user the Arbitrum One sequencer ignores can force a transaction in only after about 24 hours (sequencer docs). The OP Stack default sequencer window is 12 hours (outages). A challenge window of minutes is a bet that the sequencer includes the challenge. That is usually a safe bet, but it is a trust assumption and belongs in the spec.

Lightning: every state is both-signed

Both parties sign every Lightning commitment, and each update reveals the revocation secret for the previous one. If a party broadcasts a revoked state, its output is locked by to_self_delay and the other side "can use its revocation private key to claim all the funds" (BOLT 5). lnd's default delay is 144 to 2,016 blocks (sample-lnd.conf). A dispute asks only "is there a newer state?" The victim, or its watchtower, must act within the delay (Poon-Dryja paper). An lnd watchtower holds encrypted justice transactions and learns nothing until a breach (lnd docs). The window only has to cover "someone notices", which is why about a day is enough.

Commit now, reveal later: the completeness problem

If both sides sign a Merkle root of open positions, a claimant can later reveal only its winning leaves. An inclusion proof shows a leaf is in the tree. It does not show nothing else is. The known fixes commit to the shape of the set as well as its members.

  • Sparse Merkle trees. Each key has a fixed slot and empty slots hold a known zero, so both membership and non-membership have short proofs (Vitalik, Dahlberg et al.). With positions keyed 0..n-1 and n in the signed state, the chain sees a skipped slot.
  • Merkle sum trees. Each node carries the sum of its children, so a partial reveal must still match the committed total (Vitalik, proof of solvency). For us the sums would be locked collateral per side and open notional.
  • Nullifiers. Record a settled leaf's ID on-chain and reject any second claim with that ID (Zcash protocol spec, Tornado.sol).
  • Counter-reveal. Plasma Cash let a challenger answer an exit with history the exiter left out (ethresear.ch). Our counterparty holds every leaf and can do the same.

None of this helps a party that lost the data. Plasma broke on exactly this, since an operator could "withhold data needed to create the fraud-proof" (ethereum.org), and "not publishing data is not a uniquely attributable fault" (Vitalik). Blobs are not an archive either. Nodes must serve them for 4,096 epochs, about 18 days (Deneb p2p spec), less than a monthly option's life.

Validity proofs and TEEs

zkVMs are fast and cheap now, and they still ship soundness bugs. SP1 Hypercube proves 99.7% of L1 blocks in under 12 seconds on 16 RTX 5090s (Succinct), and went to mainnet in February 2026 with formally verified RISC-V constraints (Succinct). Verifying a Groth16 proof costs about 270k gas, PLONK about 300k (SP1 docs). Axiom has shut down its coprocessor (Trail of Bits). An accelerated ecrecover is about 120k RISC Zero cycles (risc0-crypto), and Boundless reports $0.04 per billion cycles, UNVERIFIED as I saw it only on the blog index. By my arithmetic, 100 signatures plus a margin check would cost well under a cent to prove. The bugs are the constraint. SP1 had a January 2025 flaw allowing "universal forgeries of proofs for arbitrary statements" (LambdaClass), and RISC Zero had Critical advisories in June and October 2025 (advisories). Patching means an upgradeable verifier, which means an admin key.

TEEs trust Intel, and Intel excludes physical attacks. A TDX or SGX quote proves which code ran on a genuine CPU, and "the manufacturer (Intel) is part of the chain of trust" (Flashbots). TDX "does not defend against replay of memory through physical attacks" (Intel whitepaper). In 2025 TEE.fail used an interposer costing under $1,000 to extract the key behind SGX and TDX attestation (tee.fail). A solver running its own TEE has exactly that physical access. BuilderNet's docs now say its "nodes are not running in TEEs" while it changes security model (BuilderNet). On-chain DCAP verification costs 4 to 5M gas, or about 500k behind a ZK proof (Automata).

Restaked and threshold attestation

EigenLayer. Slashing went live on 2025-04-17, opt-in per operator set (Eigen Labs). An AVS may slash "for any reason", and the fault "does not have to be objectively attributable" (ELIP-002). EigenLayer does not judge slashes and offers no veto. Slashed stake is burned unless the set uses redistribution to a fixed recipient (ELIP-006), and then whoever holds the slasher and recipient keys "can drain the entirety of … allocated stake". Slashing covers whatever the AVS's own contract can be convinced of, so it is one more dispute system to build, not a replacement for one.

Muon. Nodes produce one threshold Schnorr signature once "a predefined majority of the nodes, for example 15 out of 20" agree (Muon docs, Wayback). Our MuonOracle.verifyTSSAndGW checks it plus an ECDSA signature from one gateway, and SETTER_ROLE can swap both keys (contracts/helpers/MuonOracle.sol). Verification is two ecrecover-based checks at 3,000 gas each (execution specs), plus overhead I have not benchmarked. I found no slashing for false signatures. muon-avs-contracts at bbea100 has no slashing code. Committees like this tend to fail by key theft. Ronin lost five of nine validator keys in 2022 (post-mortem).

What this means for options-core

The ladder

Unlinked gas figures are my estimates and need a benchmark.

Evidence What the chain learns Time to final Gas Privacy Liveness needed
1. Both-signed state Both agreed to this hash at this version Next block Two ecrecover plus one storage write Hash only None to settle
2. One-party claim, bond, challenge One side said X and nobody refuted it in time The window, in hours Claim plus finalize. Disputes add reveals and engine calls per position Hash until disputed Counterparty or watchtower lands a transaction within the window
3. Third-party attestation A threshold or an enclave vouched for X Seconds Muon: a few ecrecover. DCAP: 0.5 to 5M Attester sees the data Attester network up
4. Validity proof f(committed inputs) = output Proving time plus a block 270k to 300k, shareable across a batch Inputs can stay private Any prover with the data

Rungs 3 and 4 answer computation questions, like what a position pays or whether a book is above margin. Neither answers the freshness question, "is there a newer state both sides signed?" Only the counterparty knows that, unless the newer state is already on-chain. This is the point to build around. The periodic checkpoint settles freshness. Everything up to the last checkpoint is fixed, and the window only protects trades since then. The five-to-ten-minute write is not a performance feature. It caps what a stale-state attack can win.

Who must hold what

  • Each party keeps the latest co-signed state, both signatures, every leaf under positionsRoot with its key, and the oracle data those positions settle against, or a way to fetch it later. Without this a party loses any dispute on any rung. An encrypted client backup is therefore part of the protocol.
  • A watchtower needs only the latest co-signed hash and signatures to defeat a stale claim. To answer an omission it also needs the leaves, encrypted or not.
  • An attester or prover needs the leaves at the moment it vouches and nothing afterwards.
  • The chain keeps the hash, version, position count, per-side sums, and nullifiers of settled positions. With count and sums signed, an omitted or invented leaf is visible on-chain.
  • Published data helps indexers, but blobs last about 18 days, so someone must archive a 30-day option.

Recommendation for v1

Put rungs 1 and 2 in the core. Make 3 and 4 adjudicator plug-ins.

  1. Rung 1 is the normal path. A checkpoint with both signatures applies at once.
  2. Rung 2 is the exit, built like Lightning. A unilateral claim is answerable only with a newer co-signed state or with leaves the claimant left out, checked against count and sums. A stale claim forfeits the bond to the counterparty. The challenge secures correctness, not the bond, since a stale claim can win up to the whole channel. The bond pays the challenger's gas and prices griefing.
  3. Set the window in hours. The happy path never waits on it, so its length costs nothing until a dispute. On an L2, anything shorter than the forced-inclusion delay trusts the sequencer. I would take that delay plus a day to notice, about 48 hours on Arbitrum One and 36 on a default OP Stack chain, as a per-channel parameter with a protocol floor. A shorter window is a legitimate choice if the team decides to trust the sequencer, but it should be a decision, not a default. The cost lands on margin. A solver cannot liquidate a vanished user faster than the window, so short positions need collateral for a window's worth of price movement.
  4. Plug-ins answer computation questions only and are pinned per channel at open. Either side can escalate a plug-in result to the on-chain engine before the window ends, and no plug-in can override a newer co-signed state. A plug-in saves gas and keeps leaves private when nobody escalates. Remove every plug-in and channels still settle through rungs 1 and 2, slower and more publicly.
  5. Do not let rung 3 shorten the window. Muon has no slashing I could find and our verifier trusts a gateway key and a setter role, EigenLayer slashing is whatever an AVS decides, and TEE keys fall to physical access. None of them should release funds faster than the counterparty can object.

Rung 4 is worth a prototype for private margin checks on a large book. With zkVM soundness advisories still appearing in 2026, keep it out of the v1 trust path.
