Resource locks, allocators, and intent standards

Document metadata

title: Resource locks, allocators, and intent standards
tags:
    - symmio
    - options-core
    - research
date: 2026-10-03
status: research note

This note asks one question. Can options-core keep a user's collateral in one shared pool and still promise a solver, before it hedges, that the user cannot withdraw or double-commit those funds?

Every system below that gives fillers that promise does it the same way. Some party other than the user co-signs every spend from the pool. The user can still leave without that party, but only after a fixed delay longer than any promise still outstanding. The Compact spells this out most clearly, so it gets the most space.

All sources were checked on 2026-10-03. Versions and commits are listed in Sources.

The Compact (Uniswap Labs, v1)

Mechanism

The Compact is an ownerless ERC-6909 contract. A deposit mints a token whose id combines the underlying token with a bytes12 lockTag, which packs allocatorId, Scope (Multichain or ChainSpecific) and ResetPeriod (README). ResetPeriod is an enum of eight values from OneSecond to ThirtyDays (ResetPeriod.sol). TenMinutes and OneHourAndFiveMinutes fit a "write every few minutes" rhythm.

The token owner, called the sponsor, signs an EIP-712 Compact (arbiter, sponsor, nonce, expires, lockTag, token, amount) or a BatchCompact carrying Lock[] commitments, each with an optional Mandate witness (README). The contract hashes the witness with a caller-supplied typestring and never reads it. The arbiter interprets it (README). To settle, the arbiter calls claim(Claim). The Compact checks expires, consumes the nonce in the allocator's scope, verifies the sponsor's signature or registration, calls IAllocator.authorizeClaim, and pays each Component{claimant, amount} (ClaimProcessorLib.sol). The claim hash uses msg.sender as the arbiter, so only the named arbiter can submit (HashLib.sol).

No claim needs its own lock transaction, because nothing leaves a lock without the allocator. ERC-6909 transfers call IAllocator.attest (TransferLogic.sol), withdrawals go through allocatedTransfer with allocatorData, and claims call authorizeClaim. The allocator keeps the ledger of what is promised and refuses to sign past the unallocated balance. The reference allocators show the checks that make this hold over time.

  • Smallocator refuses a compact if the lock has a forced withdrawal pending, if already-allocated plus requested exceeds the finalized balance, or if now + resetPeriod < expires (allocation.ts, domain.ts).
  • OnChainAllocator requires expires < now + minResetPeriod, and refuses if a scheduled forced withdrawal unlocks on or before expires (OnChainAllocator.sol).

The user's exit is enableForcedWithdrawal(id), a wait of resetPeriod, then forcedWithdrawal(id, recipient, amount), with no allocator signature (WithdrawalLogic.sol). Emissaries (IEmissary.verifyClaim) are fallback signers for smart accounts whose ERC-1271 logic can change, and replacing one also waits out the reset period (README).

Trust

Party Can censor Can steal Can double-spend If it disappears
Allocator Yes, new compacts and withdrawals No, it cannot move funds without the sponsor Yes, by over-signing Sponsors force-withdraw after resetPeriod. Signed compacts stay claimable only if authorizeClaim just checks a signature issued up front.
Arbiter Yes, by not submitting Yes, within what the sponsor signed. It picks claimants No Claimants cannot get paid
Sponsor No No Only if the allocator helps Not needed after signing

Sponsors "cannot unilaterally cancel a signed compact; only allocators can effectively do so by consuming the nonce" (README), via consume(nonces) (AllocatorLogic.sol). So a claimant trusts the allocator not to cancel after the claimant has acted, as well as not to over-allocate.

OpenZeppelin's audit says fillers must decide "whether a claim's condition is safe to fulfill, especially considering the reset period", and warns that sponsors can change their code with EIP-7702 (OZ audit, p. 8). Both audits found the same top bug, a storage slot that left out the sponsor, so anyone could set any user's emissary and steal from their locks (OZ H-01, Spearbit 5.1.1, fixed in 3caea0b, Spearbit audit). It was hand-written assembly slot math, a risk we inherit if we copy that style.

For options-core

  • Goal 1. The Compact is already a product-agnostic ledger. Balances sit per owner, lock and token. They move only by allocator-approved transfer or by an arbiter-submitted claim. A claim can pay out as a transfer, as a withdrawal, or by converting the balance into a lock under a different allocator (README). That last option is "pool A pays pool B" plus "B's funds now sit under another controller".
  • Goal 2. Mandate plus typestring is a generic witness. Our Rule[] can be the mandate, and our arbiter can be an immutable contract that runs the validators. That turns the arbiter, the most powerful party in The Compact, into code.
  • Goal 3. The allocator's co-signature is the off-chain lock, and it replaces lockOpenIntent. The solver is covered once it holds the sponsor signature and the allocator signature. It can submit any time before expires. Batching every few minutes only works if the batch interval and expires both fit inside the reset period.
  • A compact pays in one direction, from sponsor to claimants. An option trade locks two things, the user's premium and the solver's collateral, so both need allocating in the same step.
  • The reset period caps how long a promise can last. Option positions last days or weeks. A compact can cover the fill window. It cannot hold position margin for a week unless users accept a week-long exit.

ERC-7683 (cross-chain intents)

Mechanism

ERC-7683 is still a Draft, and the file was rewritten on 2026-05-13 ("Redesign around resolvers"). The orderData and ResolvedCrossChainOrder fields named in the brief come from the earlier draft.

That earlier draft defined GaslessCrossChainOrder with bytes32 orderDataType (an EIP-712 typehash) and opaque bytes orderData. IOriginSettler.openFor escrowed the user's funds, and resolve returned ResolvedCrossChainOrder with maxSpent, minReceived and fillInstructions (previous draft). The filler's guarantee was that escrow, ideally opened through a Permit2 witness transfer so one signature both approves tokens and binds the order (previous draft).

The current draft drops all of that. A protocol publishes an IResolver with resolve(bytes payload) view returns (ResolvedOrder), which returns steps (Call with attributes such as SpendsERC20, TimingBounds, RevertPolicy), variables, payments and Assumption{string name; bytes data} (current). Solvers run it with eth_call. A resolver "MUST guarantee that an order may only abort as explicitly specified in revert policies", and lists anything it cannot check as a named assumption for the solver to validate (current). The rationale says the old structs were "only superficially standardized", and that escrow-first did not fit "fill-first protocols" where "user funds are under a lock that is trusted by the solver" (current).

Trust

The ERC defines no settlement. The resolver is "the point of trust", and solvers whitelist resolver addresses (current). The security section names our exact problem. Audits should verify "that payment paths cannot be invalidated after the solver has incurred costs" (current). The standard leaves that guarantee to whatever lock or escrow sits underneath.

For options-core

  • A resolver is the same idea as our validators, view code on-chain that both sides run off-chain. Assumption{name, data} has nearly the same shape as Rule{validator, kind, data}.
  • The authors dropped the orderDataType + orderData envelope because it did not make solvers generic. A bare bytes4 kind has the same problem unless the validator returns something a solver can act on without knowing the kind.
  • Publishing a resolver for options intents is low priority. The draft is still moving, and our solvers are specialists.

OneBalance

Mechanism

OneBalance defines a resource lock as "a credible commitment made by a user to escrow some state conditional on particular conditions fulfilled, or an expiry time", issued by a TEE, MPC, smart account or protocol change (ethresear.ch). In production the locks were account-based, on Kernel smart accounts. An "RL Service" checked balances, "co-signs operations … and prevents double-spending", and "issues security attestations to solvers for immediate delivery" (archived docs). Once a lock was on, "transactions can be sent only via OneBalance Toolkit". A user_admin role on the RoleBasedValidator could "execute trustless rage quit in emergency case" (archived docs). The Toolkit API "was deprecated on May 18, 2026" (docs).

Trust

The RL Service co-signs every outflow, so it can censor everything and solvers rely on it fully for double-spend safety. It cannot spend alone. I could not find the delay or mechanics of the rage quit, so the exit path is unverified.

For options-core

An account-level lock needs a co-signer on everything the account does. Our vault already escrows, so an escrow-style lock like The Compact fits better. The deprecation is a reminder that the exit must work after the company running the co-signer stops.

Rhinestone

Mechanism

Rhinestone first shipped an ERC-7579 "Resource Lock Hook" that checks every account execution and needs an Orchestrator signature if the balance would drop below the locked amount (legacy docs). The Orchestrator "only co-signs transactions involving locked funds", "solvers are the only party that must trust the Orchestrator", and users have an "onchain escape hatch" (legacy docs). Rhinestone then co-built The Compact (README). Its current Warp router settles through CompactArbiter, Permit2Arbiter or SameChainArbiter, with relayers filling first and claiming later (threat model).

Trust

The allocator key is "controlled by Rhinestone", and users cannot change their emissary config without allocator approval, so "claims remain valid until expiry" (threat model). The same document flags a hole. An admin can call retireClaimAdapter() between a relayer's fill and its claim, stranding the relayer's funds. A timelock is planned, but there is "No on-chain enforcement" yet (threat model).

For options-core

The operator running the allocator is the realistic default for us too. The lesson I'd keep is the retired adapter. Any upgradeable code between fill and settlement breaks the solver's guarantee, so that path must be immutable or timelocked for longer than the longest claim window.

Across (filler guarantee only)

depositV3 pulls the input tokens into the origin SpokePool in the deposit transaction (SpokePool.sol). A relayer fills on the destination before fillDeadline, optionally as the exclusiveRelayer until exclusivityDeadline, and is repaid later (SpokePool.sol). Its guarantee is the per-order escrow, and its remaining risk is an origin reorg. This is the one-lock-transaction-per-intent model options-core wants to leave. The ERC-7683 rationale names resource locks as its replacement.

UniswapX and CoW Protocol

A UniswapX order is bound by a Permit2 witness transfer. The reactor calls permitWitnessTransferFrom with the order hash as witness, and the order's nonce is a Permit2 unordered nonce (BaseReactor.sol, LimitOrderReactor.sol). The user can cancel at any time with invalidateUnorderedNonces (SignatureTransfer.sol), or simply move the tokens. In CoW, invalidateOrder sets filledAmount[orderUid] to the maximum (GPv2Settlement.sol), and funds come from a plain allowance.

Both settle atomically, so the filler never holds half a trade on-chain. UniswapX exclusivity keeps other fillers out (ExclusivityLib.sol) and does nothing about the user. A filler that hedges before its transaction lands has no protection, which is Symmio's failure mode today.

Anoma resource machine

Mechanism

State is a set of immutable resources with logicRef, labelRef, valueRef, quantity, isEphemeral, nonce, nullifierKeyCommitment and randSeed (spec). A resource's kind is kindHash(labelRef, logicRef) (spec). The label decides fungibility, and the value holds extra data that does not. Creating a resource publishes a commitment. Consuming it publishes a nullifier, which can appear only once (spec). A transaction is valid when every created or consumed resource's logic proof passes and the delta proof shows each kind balances (spec, spec). The machine checks conservation and uniqueness and leaves meaning to the resource logic. Intents are unbalanced transactions that solvers compose into balanced ones (spec). On EVM a protocol adapter keeps the commitment tree and nullifier set and verifies RISC Zero proofs (spec, pa-evm).

Trust

The nullifier set stops double-spends at ordering time only. A user can consume the same resource in another transaction before the solver's lands, unless the resource logic demands an extra signature. Anoma has a clean generic core and no hedge-first guarantee.

For options-core

Anoma shows the right split for goals 1 and 2. The core checks per-token conservation, and checks that the logic attached to each debited pool approved the debit. It never reads application data. An allocator is that kind of logic, a per-pool rule that approves spends. The full model, with ZK proofs and commitment trees, is far more than we need.

What this means for options-core

The shared pattern

Every design that lets a solver act before settlement has the same parts. The user signs something they cannot revoke. A third party with a ledger co-signs to say the funds are not promised elsewhere. The user can leave without that party after a delay longer than any open promise.

Per-pair channels fit this pattern too. The counterparty co-signs for the pair's collateral, and the challenge period is the delay. The real choice is who does the co-signing.

(a) Per-pair channels vs (b) shared balance with an allocator

(a) Per-pair channel (b) Shared balance with an allocator
Capital Split per solver before trading One balance, quotes from any solver
Who stops double-commitment The counterparty co-signs each state The allocator co-signs each spend
What the solver trusts The contracts The allocator, for every promise it signs
What the user trusts Nobody for safety. Exit takes a challenge period The allocator for liveness. Exit takes the reset period
Lock per intent None None, one signature
Open positions Margin isolated in the pair Needs a separate home, see weaknesses
On-chain writes Checkpoints Claim batches, at least one per expires window

(b) adds a trusted party for solvers. (a) adds none.

Allocator as the matching engine, or a separate service

The RFQ server already sees every intent in order, so making it the allocator adds no new party. Rhinestone's Orchestrator did routing, intent queuing and lock accounting in one service (legacy docs). A separate allocator only needs amounts, sponsor, nonce and expiry. Smallocator signs while seeing only the witness hash, never the order (README). Separation hides order details from the allocator and lets several venues share one, at the cost of one more network hop before the solver can hedge. I'd start with the matching engine as allocator and keep the message format splittable.

Bounding the allocator's trust

  • Exit. Forced withdrawal after the reset period. Smallocator and OnChainAllocator enforce the expiry checks as allocator policy. Our vault should enforce them itself and reject a co-signed commitment whose expires falls after now + resetPeriod or after a pending forced withdrawal.
  • No silent cancel. Leave out anything like consume. After the allocator co-signs, only the solver's signature or expiry can cancel.
  • Equivocation proofs and a bond. The allocator could sign a sequence number and cumulative allocated amount per pool with each approval, the way QualifiedAllocator signs QualifiedClaim(claimHash, qualificationArg) (QualifiedAllocator.sol). Two signatures with one sequence number, or a cumulative amount above the pool balance, would prove over-allocation on-chain and could slash a bond to pay the harmed solver. This is my proposal, not something The Compact does. The bond must cover the largest total of open promises, so allocator capital caps throughput.
  • On-chain allocator logic. OnChainAllocator removes the trust but costs one transaction per allocation, the cost we are trying to remove. A middle ground is a periodic checkpoint of the allocator's ledger root with fraud proofs against it.
  • Immutable settlement path. See Rhinestone's retired adapter.

Weaknesses of (b)

  • An allocator protects the fill window, not the position. Margin shared across open positions with several solvers is cross-margin, which needs a portfolio risk engine and liquidations. An allocator provides neither.
  • If the allocator is down, nobody trades, and users wait out the reset period to exit. Ten minutes or an hour is tolerable. Thirty days is not.
  • Batch interval ≤ expires − now < resetPeriod. Longer batches force longer exits.
  • Two-sided trades need the user's premium and the solver's collateral allocated together, and the arbiter must settle both or neither.
  • EIP-7702 and ERC-1271 sponsors can change whether their signatures are valid. Note 00 already handles this with a registered plain key. An emissary that only changes with the allocator's signature also works.

Suggested direction: shared until filled, isolated once filled

Free balance sits in one pool under an allocator, and any solver can quote against it. On a fill, the allocator co-signs both sides and the solver hedges. The next batch pays the premium to the solver and moves both sides' margin into a per-pair or per-position lock. The channel or engine contract owns that lock and controls the position until expiry or close, so exit follows the channel's rules, not the reset period.

Collateral stays shared while idle, and settlement stays bilateral while at risk. The channel design in note 00 still holds. The allocator only replaces the up-front split of collateral per solver.

Open questions for Naveed:

  1. Who runs the allocator at launch, and is a bond realistic?
  2. Will users accept a 10-minute or 1-hour exit from free balance?
  3. Does any product need cross-margin across solvers, or is per-pair margin enough?
